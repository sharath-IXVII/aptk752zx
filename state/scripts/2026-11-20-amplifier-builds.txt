Building an amplifier from scratch is not just about connecting a few parts. It is about understanding the flow of electricity from the power supply to the speaker. Think of it like plumbing. Water flows from a source, through pipes, and out a faucet. Electricity does the same. It flows from the positive terminal of your battery or power supply, through the components, and back to the negative terminal. If you block the path or connect something backward, the flow stops, or worse, something pops.

Our goal today is to build a simple, reliable class AB amplifier. This type of amplifier is the standard for good sound quality and efficiency. We will start with the power supply, move to the preamp stage, and finish with the output stage that drives the speaker. Along the way, I will show you exactly how to orient every component. Getting this right is the difference between a humming, working amp and a dead circuit.

First, let's talk about the power supply. This is the heart of your amplifier. We need a transformer, a rectifier, and a filter capacitor. The transformer steps down the mains voltage to a safer level. Inside the transformer, you will see two wires coming from the secondary winding. These will connect to our rectifier. Now, for the rectifier. We will use a bridge rectifier. This component is critical. It has four terminals. Look at the body of the bridge rectifier. You will see a marking, often a line or a plus sign. This marking indicates the cathode. In a bridge rectifier configuration, the cathode (marked side) faces the **DC output/filter capacitor side** to allow conventional current flow from the AC source through the bridge to the filter capacitor. The AC from the transformer connects to the remaining two input pins. If you flip the bridge rectifier, the capacitor will charge incorrectly, and you might damage your components.

Now, let's add the filter capacitor. This smooths out the ripples in the voltage. Capacitors are not symmetric. Look at the body. You will see a stripe or a minus sign. The stripe or minus sign is the **negative side**. The other side is positive. In our circuit, the **positive side of the capacitor connects to the positive rail** (the AC rectified output side), and the negative side connects to the ground or the negative rail. If you connect an electrolytic capacitor backward, it can explode. Literally. The electrolyte inside can vent, and you will have a mess on your hands. Always connect the negative side to the lower voltage point. In our case, that is the ground rail.

With the power supply ready, we move to the preamp stage. This stage takes the weak audio signal from your source, like a phone or a MP3 player, and boosts it to a level the output stage can use. We will use a simple transistor circuit for this. The audio signal enters the base of the transistor. The emitter connects to ground. The collector connects to the positive rail through a resistor. But wait. We must bias the transistor correctly. We use two resistors to set the voltage at the base. This ensures the transistor is half-on before the signal arrives. If the bias is wrong, the transistor will sit in cutoff or saturation, and you will get massive distortion, or no sound at all. **To bias correctly, calculate the base voltage to be approximately half of your supply voltage using a voltage divider network. Choose resistor values based on Ohm's law and the transistor's base current requirements to ensure the transistor operates in the active region.**

Now, the output stage. This is where the magic happens. We need two transistors, one NPN and one PNP. They work in a push-pull configuration. The NPN transistor handles the positive half of the waveform. The PNP transistor handles the negative half. **Connect the emitter of the NPN transistor to the ground rail. Connect the collector of the NPN transistor to the positive rail.** Connect the emitter of the PNP transistor to the positive rail. Connect the collector of the PNP transistor to the speaker positive terminal (or the output junction). The collectors of both transistors connect together at the speaker input junction. The base of the NPN transistor receives the audio signal through a coupling capacitor. Without a heat sink, the transistor will fail permanently due to overheating. Solder the transistors to the heat sink using thermal paste. Do not skip this step if you want your amplifier to last.
