Welcome to the complete beginner's guide to designing professional PCBs. Right now, you have an idea, but turning that into a physical circuit board that actually works is harder than it looks. By the end of this video, you will have the exact roadmap to go from a rough sketch to a manufacturable board, and I will give you the exact commands and steps to verify your design. Let's dive in.

First, what you need. You don't need expensive software to start. You need a computer, preferably with at least 8 gigabytes of RAM, and you need design software. For this tutorial, we will use KiCad, which is free and open source. It works on Windows, Mac, and Linux. You also need a basic understanding of electricity—specifically, that conventional current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. This is the foundation we will build on.

Step 1: Install KiCad and start your first project. Go to the official KiCad website and download the version for your operating system. Run the installer and follow the prompts. Once installed, open KiCad and click 'New Project'. Name your project 'MyFirstPCB'. KiCad will create two files: a schematic file and a PCB file. This is your workspace. In the project manager, you will see these two icons. Double-click the schematic icon to open the editor. This is where you draw your circuit.

Now, the heart of any circuit is the components. We will place a resistor and an LED. In the schematic editor, find the 'Place' menu, or press the 'P' key on your keyboard. This activates the place tool. Move your cursor over the workspace and click to place the first component. For a resistor, search for 'R' in the component selector. For the LED, remember the orientation. An LED has an anode and a cathode. The anode is the side marked with a '+' or no mark in the KiCad symbol library, and it connects to positive voltage. The cathode is the side with the stripe, and it connects to ground. **Critical:** The stripe on the LED symbol points to the cathode. If you connect power to the stripe side, the LED will not light up, regardless of leg length.

Step 2: Connect the components and nets. We need to connect the resistor to the LED. In KiCad, nets are automatically created when you draw a wire. Go to the 'Place' menu again, or press 'W' for wire. Click on the output pin of the resistor and drag to the input pin of the LED. You will see a green line appear. This green line is a net. It means these two points are electrically connected. Now, we need a power source. We will use a battery. Place a voltage source symbol from the power submenu. Set the value to 5 volts. 

Connect the positive terminal of the battery to the resistor. Connect the other side of the resistor to the LED **anode** (the side without the stripe). The **cathode** of the LED (the side with the stripe) will connect to ground. In KiCad, ground is usually a net called 'GND'. Place a ground symbol and connect the LED cathode to it explicitly, ensuring the wire clicks onto the ground pad.

Step 3: Check your schematic for errors. This is the most common mistake beginners make. Go to the 'Tools' menu and select 'Check Schematic'. KiCad will run a netlist check. It will look for unconnected pins. If you see a warning, it means a pin is not connected to anything. Fix that by drawing a wire to the correct pin. If the check passes, you have a valid schematic. This is the blueprint for your board.

Now, we move to the PCB design. Go back to the project manager and double-click the PCB editor icon. This is where we turn the schematic into a physical board. Step 4: Import the netlist. In the PCB editor, go to the 'File' menu and select 'Import'. Choose 'Netlist from KiCad Project'. KiCad will read your schematic and prepare the component list. **Note:** The components will appear on the board, but they are not yet placed or routed; you must position them manually. They are not connected yet; we will add the copper traces.

Step 5: Place components and define the board outline. Click on a component and move it. Drag it to a convenient location. Remember, in a real PCB, we have a rectangular board outline. Go to the 'Place' menu, select 'Edge.Cuts'. This is the outline of your board. Click to place corners. For a simple beginner board, make it 50 millimeters by 30 millimeters. Leave space around the edges so you can hold the board later.

Step 6: Route the traces. This is the part most people get wrong. Traces are the copper paths that connect your components. In the PCB editor, go to the 'Route' tool, or press 'B' for board routing. Click on a pad, and drag to the next pad. You will see a yellow line following your mouse. KiCad has a default trace width. For power lines, we want wider traces, about 0.5 millimeters. For signal lines, 0.2 millimeters is standard. You can change the width in the properties panel. Route the trace from the resistor to the LED anode. Notice that traces should not cross if possible. If they must cross, use a via. A via is a hole that allows a trace to go to another layer. To add a via, hover over the intersection of two nets and press 'V'. A via will be placed, and you can route the trace over it. Ensure the via is electrically connected to both nets by checking the net labels appear on the via.

Step 7: Add copper pour for ground. This is a pro tip. Instead of just thin traces, we can fill empty space with copper connected to ground. This helps with noise and heat. Go to the 'Draw' menu and select 'Polygon pour'. In the settings, set the net to 'GND'. Click around the components, ensuring the polygon pour is assigned to the GND net. KiCad will fill the area with a solid copper region. This is your ground plane. **Important:** Ensure the clearance around the ground pour is correctly set in the design rules to avoid thin copper slivers.

Step 8: Generate the manufacturing files. Once your board looks good, we need to send it to a fab. Go to the 'File' menu and select 'Plot'. In the plot dialog, select 'All layers'. Make sure 'Mirror' is checked for the bottom layer if you are looking at the board from the bottom side. Click 'Plot'. This creates PDF files. Next, go to 'File' and select 'Generate Drill File'. This creates the files for the machine that drills holes for your components. These two files—the Gerber files and the drill file— are what you send to a PCB manufacturer.

How do you check it worked? After you send the files to a manufacturer, you receive the physical board. To test it, use a multimeter. Set it to continuity mode. Touch the probes to the LED pins. **Do not rely on continuity mode alone to light the LED.** To verify the LED lights, connect the battery to the circuit. If the LED lights up when you connect the battery, your design worked. If it does not light up, check your connections.

Now, fixes for the two most common errors. Error one: The LED is backwards. Remember, the cathode stripe must face away from positive voltage. If your LED does not light, flip it 180 degrees. Error two: Unconnected nets. If your multimeter shows no continuity, check your schematic check warnings. Often, a pin is not connected to a wire. Redraw the wire ensuring it clicks onto the pad.

Here is the part most people get wrong: They forget the ground connection. Without a solid ground connection, your circuit will behave erratically or not work at all. Always ensure your ground plane is connected to every component that needs it, and that the ground net is explicitly tied to the power source's negative terminal.

Let's do a quick recap. We installed KiCad, created a schematic, placed a resistor and LED with correct orientation (cathode stripe to ground), checked the netlist, imported to the PCB editor, placed components manually, routed traces, added a ground pour with correct clearance, and generated manufacturing files. The most important rule: always check the anode and cathode of your LED (stripe to ground), ensure every pin is connected with a wire that clicks onto the pad, and ensure the ground plane is solid and connected.
